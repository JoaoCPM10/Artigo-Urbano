\title{Directed Structure Reveals an Access Bottleneck that
Undirected Analysis Misses: A Graph Study of Three Rio de Janeiro
Neighbourhoods}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\authorrunning{Anonymous}
\institute{Affiliation withheld for double-blind review}

\begin{abstract}
This exploratory study models the street networks of three Rio de Janeiro
neighbourhoods as directed, weighted graphs from OpenStreetMap, asking
whether \emph{directed} structure reveals an access bottleneck that
undirected analysis misses, and whether it reflects geography rather than
size. The resulting route count could help a mobility or civil-defence
authority flag a neighbourhood with only one way out. Using classical
algorithms (connectivity, Dijkstra,
Bellman--Ford, betweenness centrality, Edmonds--Karp maximum flow), we
compare a compact peninsula (Urca) with two large, open beachfront grids
(Copacabana and Botafogo). Structural indicators separate both grids from
Urca but, once a bounding-box error that misattributed part of Botafogo to
Copacabana is corrected, no longer resemble each other closely.
Node-to-node minimum cut saturates at one everywhere; a maximum flow from
each core to its real administrative gateways instead orders the cases:
one gateway for Urca, eighteen for Copacabana, sixteen for Botafogo. The
bottleneck is thus specific to the peninsula's geography, not its size,
and does \emph{not} generalise to large neighbourhoods. All code and data
are open and reproducible.

\keywords{Urban mobility \and Directed graphs \and Strong connectivity
\and Betweenness centrality \and Maximum flow \and Street networks
\and OpenStreetMap.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Street networks are naturally modelled as graphs whose vertices are
intersections and whose edges are street segments. Whether the model is
\emph{directed} matters: one-way regulation can leave an intersection
reachable but offer no way back, a distinction that an undirected model
cannot express. This paper is an exploratory study that characterises the
street networks of three neighbourhoods of Rio de Janeiro and tests one
guiding question: does the directed structure reveal an access bottleneck
that the undirected view misses, and is that bottleneck a property of the
neighbourhood's \emph{geography} rather than of its \emph{size}?

To separate geography from size we compare a compact, geographically
constrained neighbourhood, Urca (a peninsula reached from the rest of the
city through a single corridor), against two large, open beachfront grids,
Copacabana and Botafogo. The two large grids act as comparison cases: if
Urca's pattern were merely an artefact of small scale, the metrics would
line up with size; if it is geographic, Urca should stand apart from both
large grids. Copacabana is of particular interest because its access to
the rest of the city is popularly associated with a small number of
tunnels, which makes it a plausible candidate for a severe structural
bottleneck.

Beyond the algorithmic question, the access flow has a direct practical
reading: the number of independent routes a neighbourhood would lose
before becoming unreachable by street, useful when planning around a
flood, a landslide, or a tunnel closure.

The contributions are: (i) a fully reproducible pipeline that builds
directed, weighted street graphs from OpenStreetMap, cross-checks their
extent against official administrative boundaries, and analyses their
connectivity, centrality and flow; (ii) a comparative characterisation of
three real neighbourhoods showing that the standard structural indicators
do \emph{not} distinguish them along a bottleneck axis; and (iii) the
observation that a maximum flow from each neighbourhood's core to its real
administrative gateways \emph{does} distinguish them, unlike node-to-node
minimum cut. We deliberately keep the analysis to $n=3$ contrasting cases and read
the results as illustrative, not as a statistically generalisable claim.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Extracting a routable graph from OpenStreetMap and simplifying its
topology (contracting the shape points that are mere bends of a street into
single edges between true intersections) is standard practice, popularised
by tools such as OSMnx~\cite{boeing}; we follow the same construction and
simplification in a compact, reproducible pipeline, verified against the
\texttt{networkx} library.

Connectivity has a classical toolkit for \emph{undirected} graphs: bridges and
articulation points, computed in linear time by Tarjan's depth-first
search~\cite{tarjan1972depth}, identify the edges and vertices whose removal
disconnects the network. These primitives answer whether two points remain
\emph{mutually reachable} when a street closes, but they are blind to
direction. Italiano et al.~\cite{italiano2012finding} generalised them to
directed graphs as \emph{strong} bridges and \emph{strong} articulation
points, whose removal increases the number of strongly connected components,
and gave linear-time algorithms; the strong notions capture whether one can
travel \emph{and return}, not merely reach.

The distinction is not academic for street networks, where one-way regulation
is pervasive (74--93\% of segments in our cases). Tools such as
OSMnx~\cite{boeing} build directed street graphs, yet connectivity and
resilience studies commonly reduce them to the undirected graph or to aggregate
topological summaries~\cite{corcoran2021persistent}, conflating ``reachable''
with ``reachable and returnable'' and hiding the effect of direction. We apply
the strong notions to real neighbourhoods, report the weak (undirected) and
strong pictures side by side, and quantify how much the strongly connected core
shrinks under directedness --- a gap the undirected analysis cannot see. We
favour transparency over asymptotics: rather than Italiano's linear-time
algorithm, we recompute strong components after removing each candidate
element, restricted to the giant strong core, which runs in seconds at
this scale (tens to a few hundred intersections), a simplicity choice, not
a scalability claim.

A large body of work locates critical points in street networks through
centrality, especially betweenness~\cite{kirkley2018betweenness,halavachou2021uncovering},
and through models of street bottlenecks~\cite{stepanchuk2020modelling}. A
much older tradition, space syntax, reads accessibility from configurational
measures such as integration and choice computed on the axial or segment
map of a settlement~\cite{hillier1984social}. Both traditions summarise a
network into a single score per vertex; neither directly answers how many
\emph{independent} routes connect two regions, the question that matters
before closing a street. We
instead read bottlenecks through maximum flow / minimum cut, which counts
street-disjoint routes explicitly and is comparable in spirit to, but
coarser-grained than, either aggregate approach.

Resilience of urban networks has been studied as the degradation of
service under failure. Ganin et al.~\cite{ganin2017resilience}, across
forty US urban areas, find that efficiency and resilience vary
independently; our comparison cannot test that claim directly, but is
consistent with it, since Urca is not inefficient yet is by far the most
structurally fragile of the three cases. Topology-based assessments simulate random and
targeted failures to rank critical elements~\cite{rouhana2020,alatristasalas2019};
Ientile et al.~\cite{ientile2022measuring} formalise a loss-of-serviceability
index; and a topological approach~\cite{corcoran2021persistent} describes the
shape of road networks. We measure resilience concretely, as the number
of origin--destination pairs that lose all connection when a single strong
element is removed, and we relate the cost of a forced detour to the
alternative-route literature~\cite{silver2018tuned}. Against this
background, most analyses either treat the network as undirected or
summarise it through an aggregate score; few isolate the effect of
\emph{directedness} on access connectivity and contrast it across
neighbourhoods of distinct geography, which is the gap this paper explores.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data and Graph Construction}
\label{sec:data}

For each neighbourhood we query the drivable ways (\texttt{highway} of
motorway to residential classes) inside a bounding box from the
OpenStreetMap Overpass API, and cache the raw response so that the analysis
is reproducible offline. Each street segment becomes a directed arc
respecting its \texttt{oneway} tag: a two-way street yields two opposite
arcs, a one-way street a single arc. Degree-two shape points are contracted,
so that vertices are true intersections and edges are the streets between
them; without this step every bend of a street would become a spurious
articulation point. Coordinates are projected to metres and each edge is
weighted by its travel time, computed from its length and a default speed
per road class when OpenStreetMap gives no \texttt{maxspeed}. Figure~%
\ref{fig:construction} illustrates the conversion from map to directed
graph.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/fig_construction.png}
\caption{From map to directed graph: intersections become vertices and
street segments become arcs; a one-way street yields a single arc (arrow),
a two-way street two opposite arcs.}
\label{fig:construction}
\end{figure}

A hand-drawn bounding box is a modelling choice, and we found it to be a
consequential one. Copacabana's box was originally drawn to keep its two
road tunnels as internal edges, extending north past the administrative
border with Botafogo. Cross-checking every intersection against the
official neighbourhood polygon (Instituto Pereira Passos / Data.Rio,
``Limite de Bairros''~\cite{ipp_bairros}) showed that 158 of its 496
intersections, and 155 of its street segments, in fact lay inside
Botafogo's own territory: the two published networks shared 143
identical OpenStreetMap intersections. We therefore use the hand-drawn box
only as a search envelope, unioned with the official polygon's own bounding
box to guarantee full coverage, and clip the resulting graph to the
official polygon before any analysis. Urca and Botafogo needed no such
correction: at most four of their intersections fall outside their own
polygon, and none of their published numbers change. Every Copacabana
figure and table in this paper reflects the corrected, polygon-clipped
network of 345 intersections (Table~\ref{tab:redes}), not the original 496.

\subsection{Connectivity, Shortest Paths, and Flow}
\label{sec:algos}

All analyses run over a directed, weighted adjacency-list graph, and our
implementations were verified against the \texttt{networkx} library.
Undirected connected components and directed strongly connected components
(SCC) are found with breadth-first search, the latter by intersecting the
reachable set in the graph with the reachable set in its reverse.
\emph{Weak} bridges and articulation points are computed directly from
their definition, removing the element and recounting undirected
components. \emph{Strong} bridges and articulation points are obtained
analogously in the directed setting: an element is strong when its removal
splits the giant strongly connected core into more than one SCC, following
the definitions of Italiano et al.~\cite{italiano2012finding}. Shortest
paths use Dijkstra~\cite{dijkstra1959note} with a binary heap, independently
verified by Bellman--Ford~\cite{bellman1958routing}; an alternative route
is the shortest path recomputed after blocking one segment
(Section~\ref{sec:res-resil}). Maximum
flow and minimum cut use Edmonds--Karp~\cite{edmonds1972theoretical} with
unit capacity per street, so that the maximum flow between two points
equals the number of street-disjoint routes between them and the minimum
cut is the smallest set of streets whose closure separates them.
Betweenness centrality is computed with Brandes'
algorithm~\cite{brandes2001faster} on the directed graph, weighting
shortest paths by travel time, so that a vertex's score is the fraction of
minimum-time origin--destination pairs routed through it.

\subsection{Gateway Access Flow}
\label{sec:access-flow}

The minimum cut between two single intersections turns out to saturate
at one in all three networks (Section~\ref{sec:results}): because
one-way-dense grids contain undirected bridges almost everywhere, nearly
every origin--destination pair is separated by a single edge close to an
endpoint, long before any geographic constriction.

To measure access to the rest of the city, rather than an arbitrary
internal crossing, we distinguish a neighbourhood's \emph{interior} from
its \emph{gateways}. Each street network is extracted with a small buffer
beyond the neighbourhood's own boundary (its official polygon for
Copacabana, its query bounding box for Urca and Botafogo), large enough to
include the first intersection beyond the boundary on every street that
crosses it. A vertex is a \emph{gateway} if at least one of its incident
edges crosses that boundary; every other vertex is \emph{interior}. The
gateway set is therefore the set of points through which one can
physically leave the neighbourhood, not an internal landmark chosen by a
distance heuristic. Because a gateway may admit traffic in only one
direction, we restrict the interior and the gateway set to the
neighbourhood's largest \emph{undirected} component, not its strongly
connected core: a one-way exit is still a real way out.

The source region is the fraction $f$ ($f=0.2$) of interior intersections
farthest, in number of edges, from the nearest gateway, that is, the
neighbourhood's core. A super-source feeds the source region and the full
gateway set feeds a super-sink, both with infinite capacity, and
Edmonds--Karp is run between them over the unit-capacity network. The
resulting value, which we call the \emph{access flow}, is the number of
street-disjoint routes between the neighbourhood's core and its actual
exits to the rest of the city (Section~\ref{sec:res-access}); it is stable
under $f \in [0.15, 0.25]$ for all three neighbourhoods, and, for Urca, under
a boundary search margin between 200 and 800\,m.

This replaces an earlier definition built from two internal poles found by
a graph double sweep, which measured the narrowest crossing along the
neighbourhood's longest axis but never referenced its actual boundary; we
keep that construction only as an internal cross-check that the source
region and gateway set never overlap.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Structural Indicators Do Not Separate the Cases}
\label{sec:res-struct}

Table~\ref{tab:estrutural} reports the directed structural indicators as
proportions, since the raw counts are not comparable across such different
sizes. Both large grids remain far from Urca's regime, but they no longer
track each other closely once Copacabana is corrected to its official
boundary (Section~\ref{sec:data}): Botafogo has considerably more strong
bridges (70.4\% of its core streets) and strong articulations (63.9\%) than
Copacabana (45.3\% and 54.1\%). Urca does not stand out decisively on these
proportions either (67.3\% strong bridges, 75.7\% strong articulations).
Figure~\ref{fig:urca-strong} shows, for Urca, that the strong elements
concentrate inside the residential grid rather than on a single access
point. The proportions therefore separate Urca from both open grids, but
not the two open grids from each other, a contrast developed further in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Node-to-Node Minimum Cut Saturates}
\label{sec:res-mincut}

For a well-connected origin and destination inside the strong core, the
Edmonds--Karp minimum cut equals one in all three neighbourhoods. The single
cut edge lies near one endpoint rather than at any geographic narrowing:
because the undirected network has bridges throughout, an origin--destination
pair almost always has a single separating street close to it. Node-to-node
minimum cut is therefore uninformative about access; it measures a local
bridge, not the neighbourhood's connection to the rest of the city.

\subsection{Gateway Access Flow Separates the Cases}
\label{sec:res-access}

The gateway access flow of Section~\ref{sec:access-flow} does order the
cases (Table~\ref{tab:acesso}). Urca has a single street-disjoint route
between its core and its four real gateways to the rest of the city, and
this value is stable for $f \in [0.15,0.25]$ and for a boundary search
margin between 200 and 800\,m. Copacabana offers eighteen such routes
through twenty-one gateways, stable across the same range of $f$; Botafogo
offers fifteen to sixteen through twenty-eight, the only one of the three
values with any sensitivity to $f$ at all, and still an order of magnitude
above Urca throughout. This is the only indicator that isolates an access
bottleneck, and it does not follow size: Botafogo (6--8 times larger than
Urca) and the now much smaller, post-correction Copacabana both offer an
order of magnitude more gateway routes than Urca. Copacabana's access is
not reduced to its two tunnels: they are only two of its twenty-one
gateways, so surface streets carry most of its eighteen disjoint routes.

\subsection{Betweenness Concentrates on One Intersection in Urca}
\label{sec:res-betweenness}

Directed betweenness centrality, weighted by travel time, is consistent
with the access-flow reading across all three neighbourhoods
(Figure~\ref{fig:betweenness}). In Urca a single intersection lies on 52\%
of all minimum-time origin--destination pairs, the one at the neck of the
access corridor. Copacabana and Botafogo have no such dominant vertex:
their most central intersections reach 27.8\% and 22.5\% respectively,
with the load spread along a Y-shaped arterial spine in Copacabana and
along parallel spines in Botafogo. Copacabana's figure sits somewhat above
Botafogo's, one more indicator on which the two open grids no longer track
each other exactly once Copacabana is corrected
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). We report the maximum rather than a
concentration index because the latter is confounded by core size (a small
core raises the median and lowers inequality regardless of geography); the
maximum, already normalised by the number of pairs, is comparable across
sizes and again isolates Urca.

\subsection{Resilience of the Strong Core}
\label{sec:res-resil}

We measure resilience exhaustively, closing each strong bridge or strong
articulation in turn and counting, over all ordered pairs of the core, how
many lose all connection. In Urca (1\,332 core pairs), the median strong
bridge disconnects 138 pairs and the worst 644; the median strong
articulation disconnects 219 and the worst 694. In Copacabana (109\,230
pairs on the corrected network) the median strong bridge disconnects 660
pairs and the worst 14\,280; in Botafogo (115\,940 pairs), 1\,016 and
18\,122. The \emph{fraction} of the core isolated is much higher in Urca
(a median of 10.4\% for strong bridges against 0.6\% and 0.9\% in the large
grids), but this is partly a size effect: a single removed element cuts a
larger share of a small core, so the absolute counts and the core size in
Table~\ref{tab:redes} should be read together. The qualitative point is
robust: in every neighbourhood no strong bridge is harmless, the least
damaging one still isolates a non-trivial number of pairs, and the impact
of an element is not a fixed property but depends on which pair is
considered.

Beyond binary connectivity, a forced detour has a measurable cost.
Recomputing the shortest route after blocking one non-bridge segment on it
increases travel time by 13.6\% in Urca, 5.1\% in Copacabana, and 6.0\% in
Botafogo. The same routes also cross segments that are themselves strong
bridges (3, 4, and 1 respectively): closing any of those admits no detour
at all, only the disconnection already quantified above.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read together, the indicators still separate Urca from both open grids, but
no longer group Copacabana and Botafogo as closely as an uncorrected
reading would suggest. Urca is the \emph{constrained} case: 93.3\% of its
segments are one-way, this directedness removes 38.3\% of its intersections
from the strong core, and a single gateway-disjoint route connects its core
to the rest of the city, one intersection lying on 52\% of its minimum-time
routes. Copacabana and Botafogo are both \emph{open} cases in the sense
that matters for access: neither shows anything resembling Urca's
single-gateway pattern, offering eighteen and sixteen gateway-disjoint
routes respectively, and both have a similarly high share of one-way
segments (74.9\% and 76.4\%). They are not, however, structural twins:
once Copacabana's network is corrected to its official administrative
boundary, its strong-bridge and strong-articulation proportions (45.3\%
and 54.1\%) fall well below Botafogo's (70.4\% and 63.9\%,
Table~\ref{tab:estrutural}), even though the raw one-way share barely
moved. The access bottleneck remains a property of Urca's
geography, a peninsula with one corridor, and does not generalise to a
large neighbourhood of either profile.

The most counter-intuitive result concerns Copacabana. Its access is
popularly associated with a few tunnels, which made it a plausible
candidate for a severe structural bottleneck. Yet at this resolution the
tunnel reputation does not translate into a measurable one: of the
twenty-one gateways found on its corrected boundary, the two tunnels are
only part of the count, and surface streets carry the rest of its eighteen
gateway-disjoint routes to the rest of the city. A three-step reading of
small, large, and no bottleneck is therefore not supported by the data; a
two-way contrast of constrained versus open is.

Several threats to validity temper these observations. The study rests on
$n=3$ contrasting cases and is illustrative, not statistically general.
Comparing neighbourhoods of distinct geography requires a spatial extent
that is itself a modelling choice, and that choice is not free of risk: a
hand-drawn bounding box for Copacabana placed 158 of its 496 intersections
inside Botafogo's official territory (Section~\ref{sec:data}); every
Copacabana number here reflects the corrected network, and we regard the
discrepancy as a caution for any study that defines a neighbourhood by a
hand-drawn box near a shared border. Urca and Botafogo needed no such
correction. The gateway definition depends on a fixed boundary search
margin, tested from 200 to 800\,m around Urca without any change in its
access flow but not yet repeated on the corrected Copacabana network. Unit
capacity treats every street alike, ignoring width and number of lanes;
travel times rest on default speeds where OpenStreetMap gives none; the
connectivity results depend directly on the quality of the \texttt{oneway}
tags, which are dense in these neighbourhoods (up to 93.3\% of Urca's
segments) and therefore consequential; and the betweenness maximum in
Table~\ref{tab:acesso} is not yet checked against a null model, so we
cannot rule out that part of the gap between Urca and the two open grids
reflects core size rather than geography alone
(Section~\ref{sec:res-betweenness}). Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} summarises the normalised
indicators, updated for the corrected Copacabana network.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The standard structural indicators, strong bridges and strong articulation
points, do not separate the cases along a bottleneck axis: both open grids remain far
from Urca's regime, but, once Copacabana's network is corrected to its
official administrative boundary, the two open grids no longer resemble
each other as closely as an uncorrected reading would suggest. Node-to-node
minimum cut is uninformative here, saturating at one because one-way-dense
grids contain undirected bridges almost everywhere. The distinguishing
signal comes from a maximum flow between each neighbourhood's core and its
real administrative gateways: the peninsula connects to the rest of the
city through a single street-disjoint gateway, whereas Copacabana offers
eighteen and Botafogo sixteen. A forced detour around a closed street also
costs more in Urca (13.6\% extra travel time) than in either open grid
(5.1\% and 6.0\%). The access bottleneck is thus a property of the
peninsula's geography and does not generalise to large neighbourhoods, not
even to one reputed for tunnel-constrained access. These observations rest
on $n=3$ contrasting cases and are illustrative, not statistically general;
extending the comparison to more neighbourhoods, correlating access flow
with a geometric shape index, and checking the betweenness maximum against
a null model (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) are left for future work.
